SECTION 1: THE PROBLEM WE ARE SOLVING
================================================================================

1.1  Background & Policy Context
---------------------------------
India operates a national air quality monitoring network called CAAQMS (Continuous
Ambient Air Quality Monitoring Stations), maintained by the Central Pollution
Control Board (CPCB). These ground stations measure PM2.5, PM10, NO2, and other
pollutants in real-time.

However, there are two major problems with this network:

PROBLEM 1 â€” SPARSE COVERAGE (The Spatial Deficit)
    Indian cities are massive, but ground stations are extremely few. A city like
    Vadodara may have only 1-2 operational stations, almost exclusively placed in
    industrial zones (e.g., Nandesari GIDC). This means residential areas,
    schools, and hospitals â€” where citizens actually breathe â€” have zero coverage.
    There is no sensor, no data, and no warning system for millions of people.

    This is called the "Virtual Sensing Problem": can a model predict pollution
    concentrations at locations where no physical sensor exists?

PROBLEM 2 â€” PEAK SMOOTHING (The Forecasting Failure)
    Standard deep learning models (LSTM, GRU, Transformer) trained with Mean
    Squared Error (MSE) loss have a well-documented failure mode: they predict
    near the historical mean. When pollution surges during a severe winter smog
    event (e.g., Delhi PM2.5 spiking to 800+ Âµg/mÂ³), these models predict
    something like 250 Âµg/mÂ³ â€” catastrophically underestimating a deadly event.

    This happens because MSE punishes large errors from the mean by squaring
    them, so the model "plays it safe" by staying close to average. For public
    health, this failure is unacceptable. A system that cannot predict the most
    dangerous events is not a warning system â€” it is a liability.

1.2  What We Are Targeting
---------------------------
Our system targets PM2.5 prediction (the most health-critical pollutant, directly
linked to NCAP â€” National Clean Air Programme â€” targets for 2025-2026).

We aim to:
    - Provide hourly PM2.5 forecasts from T+1 to T+24 (a full 24-hour window)
    - Cover unmonitored locations (zero-shot virtual sensing)
    - Accurately capture extreme peak events without smoothing them away
    - Run efficiently on edge hardware (no supercomputer required)
    - Use only freely available, open-source data sources

2.2  Physics Feature Engineering
----------------------------------
Raw weather data is transformed into physically meaningful features:

    TRIGONOMETRIC WIND VECTORS:
        Raw wind direction (0Â°â€“360Â°) has a boundary discontinuity: 359Â° and 1Â°
        are adjacent but numerically far apart. We convert to:
            U_wind = WindSpeed Ã— cos(direction Ã— Ï€/180)
            V_wind = WindSpeed Ã— sin(direction Ã— Ï€/180)
        These continuous vectors allow the model to properly learn directional
        pollution transport (e.g., which direction brings industrial plumes).

    INVERSE PLANETARY BOUNDARY LAYER HEIGHT (1/PBLH):
        The PBLH defines how far pollution can vertically disperse. A collapsing
        PBLH (typical in cold winter mornings) means pollution is trapped in a
        thin layer near the ground, causing concentration spikes.
        By using 1/PBLH, large values directly signal to the model that
        dispersion is suppressed and concentrations will rise sharply.

    CYCLIC TIME ENCODING:
        Hour of day (0â€“23) and month (1â€“12) are encoded as:
            hour_sin = sin(2Ï€ Ã— hour / 24)
            hour_cos = cos(2Ï€ Ã— hour / 24)
        This gives the model a continuous, boundary-free sense of diurnal and
        seasonal patterns.

    UPWIND FRP (Fire Radiative Power):
        A proxy for biomass burning (crop fires, forest fires) that can
        dramatically spike pollution when fires are upwind of the station.

SECTION 3: METHODOLOGY â€” THE TWO-STAGE DECOUPLED ARCHITECTURE
================================================================================

The fundamental design insight of this project is that the Sentinel-5P satellite
provides only ONE data point per day (~1:30 PM), but we need 24 hourly predictions.
Feeding a single daily satellite reading into a model trying to predict hourly
dynamics creates a fundamental temporal mismatch.

Our solution: DECOUPLE the problem into two specialized stages.

3.1  Stage 1: Daily Spatial Baseline Estimator
------------------------------------------------
    INPUT:  Daily Sentinel-5P NO2 VCD + daily meteorology averages + static land-use
    MODEL:  Random Forest / LightGBM spatial regressor
    OUTPUT: Daily mean PM2.5 concentration for the forecast day (one number)
    SCRIPT: src/generate_stage1_daily.py

    This stage answers the question: "What is the AVERAGE pollution level
    expected across the city for tomorrow, given what the satellite sees today?"

    The output of Stage 1 is the "daily_baseline" â€” a physically grounded
    constraint that caps the spatial background level for the day.

3.2  Stage 2: Hourly Sequence Generator (The Neural Network)
--------------------------------------------------------------
    INPUT:  24-hour window of hourly weather forecasts (T+1 to T+24)
            â†’ temperature, humidity, U_wind, V_wind, inv_PBLH, FRP, etc.
            + Static land-use features (elevation, NDVI, etc.)
            + Stage 1 daily_baseline constraint (passed as a constant feature)
    MODEL:  SpatioTemporalHybridNet (custom PyTorch architecture)
    OUTPUT: A sequence of 24 hourly RATIOS (e.g., [0.66x, 6.72x, 5.33x, ...])

    The final prediction is: y_pred = predicted_ratio Ã— daily_baseline

    This is the MULTIPLICATIVE CONSTRAINT mechanism. The network never predicts
    raw Âµg/mÂ³ values. Instead, it predicts how much HIGHER or LOWER each hour
    will be compared to the daily spatial average. This is physically grounded:
    if the satellite says the day's background is 126 Âµg/mÂ³ and the model
    detects a boundary layer collapse at T+11, it outputs a 6.72x ratio â†’
    126 Ã— 6.72 = ~846 Âµg/mÂ³ at the surface for that hour.

3.3  The Neural Network Architecture (SpatioTemporalHybridNet)
----------------------------------------------------------------
    The network has a dual-path design:

    PATH A â€” TEMPORAL DYNAMICS (CNN + BiLSTM + Attention):
        Handles the 10 dynamic hourly features.

        1D-CNN Layer (64 filters, kernel=3):
            Extracts local temporal patterns across adjacent hours. Identifies
            short-term patterns like "temperature dropped 3Â° in the last 2 hours"
            as a single learned feature. The convolution operates across time.

        BiLSTM (2 layers, hidden=128, bidirectional â†’ 256 output):
            Processes the CNN output sequentially, both forward and backward.
            The forward pass captures causal dependencies (past â†’ future).
            The backward pass captures anti-causal context (the model "knows"
            from the 24-hour forecast window what happens later in the day, which
            helps calibrate early-morning predictions).

        ResidualAttention Layer:
            Standard softmax attention crushes the signal magnitude (each
            timestep gets ~1/24 = 4% weight). Our custom residual form is:
                attended = encoder_output + encoder_output Ã— softmax_weights
            This PRESERVES 100% of the BiLSTM signal while adding a bounded
            [0, 2x] attention refinement. Critical timesteps (high inv_PBLH
            hours) get amplified, but the base signal is never lost.

    PATH B â€” STATIC CONTEXT (Late Fusion):
        The 6 static land-use features (elevation, NDVI, etc.) are constant
        across the 24 hours. They are concatenated directly with the temporal
        output at the final layer, not processed through the CNN/LSTM.
        This "late fusion" ensures static context informs the output head
        without contaminating the temporal feature extraction.

    OUTPUT HEAD (Time-Distributed Regressor):
        A 2-layer MLP applied independently to each of the 24 timesteps:
            Linear(262 â†’ 64) â†’ ReLU â†’ Dropout(0.2) â†’ Linear(64 â†’ 1)
        The output passes through Softplus (ensures ratio > 0 always).
        The ratio is then multiplied by daily_baseline to give final Âµg/mÂ³.

3.4  The Loss Function: Frequency-Weighted MAE (fMAE)
------------------------------------------------------
    Standard MSE Loss problem: treats a 400 Âµg/mÂ³ event the same as a 50 Âµg/mÂ³
    event proportionally. Since extreme events are rare (few training samples),
    the model learns to ignore them and predict the mean.

    Our custom FrequencyWeightedMAE (losses.py) calculates:
        L_fMAE = mean(w(y_true) Ã— |y_pred - y_true|)

    Where the weight w(y_true) is computed using the effective number formula:
        w = (1 - Î²) / (1 - Î²^n(y))
        Î² = 0.99 (tuned), n(y) = count of samples in PM2.5 bin

    AQI-aligned bins used: <30, 30-60, 60-90, 90-120, 120-250, 250-400, >400 Âµg/mÂ³

    Rare extreme events (PM2.5 > 400) get near-infinite weight relative to
    common events (PM2.5 = 30-60). This forces the gradient to massively
    penalise the model for getting extreme peaks wrong, making it physically
    impossible for the model to simply predict the mean and walk away.

    Additionally, a Variance Penalty term was added:
        L_total = L_fMAE + 0.3 Ã— |std(y_pred) - std(y_true)|
    This directly penalises "flat-line" predictions that match the mean but
    have no temporal variation, eliminating the last mode of peak-smoothing.

3.5  Training Configuration
-----------------------------
    Optimizer: AdamW (lr=1e-4, weight_decay=1e-4)
    Gradient Clipping: max_norm=1.0 (prevents gradient explosions)
    Early Stopping: patience=50 epochs (stops if val loss doesn't improve)

4.1  Data Split Strategy
--------------------------
The 11 station-chunks were split GEOGRAPHICALLY (by city), not randomly:

    TRAINING SET (8 chunks â€” model saw this data):
        Delhi_C14, Delhi_C15, Kolkata_C20, Kolkata_C21,
        Hyderabad_C19, Hyderabad_C20, Ahmedabad_C15, Ahmedabad_C16

        Maninagar Ahmedabad Gpcb_C18

    TEST SET / ZERO-SHOT (2 chunks â€” NEVER seen during training):
        Lucknow_C1, Patna_C20

Why geographic splitting? Random splitting would allow the model to see
one hour of a Lucknow day in training and then predict the next hour in
testing â€” this is temporal leakage that inflates results. By holding out
ENTIRE cities, we simulate the real-world use case: deploying the model
to a completely new, unmonitored city with no prior ground data.

This is aligned with the Leave-One-Station-Out (LOSO) cross-validation
protocol recommended for spatial air quality modelling in the literature.

4.3  Metrics Used
------------------
    RÂ² (Coefficient of Determination):
        Measures what fraction of variance in PM2.5 the model explains.
        RÂ² = 1.0 is perfect. RÂ² = 0.0 means the model is as good as predicting
        the mean every time. Negative values mean the model is worse than
        predicting the mean.

    MAE (Mean Absolute Error):
        Average absolute difference between predicted and actual PM2.5.
        Directly interpretable: "on average, predictions are off by X Âµg/mÂ³".
        Lower is better.

    RMSE (Root Mean Squared Error):
        Like MAE but squares the errors first, then takes the root.
        Large errors are penalised more heavily. A much higher RMSE vs MAE
        indicates the model makes some large catastrophic mistakes.
        Lower is better.

4.4  Ablation Design
----------------------
The ablation compares two hypotheses head-to-head on the SAME zero-shot test set:

    HYPOTHESIS A (Stage 1 Only):
        Use just the daily_baseline as the prediction for all 24 hours.
        y_baseline = [daily_mean_pm25] Ã— 24 (flat line)
        This represents: "What if we only had satellite data and no neural network?"

    HYPOTHESIS B (Stage 2 Hybrid):
        Use the full model: y_pred = network_ratio Ã— daily_baseline
        This represents: "What does adding the CNN/BiLSTM/Attention actually give us?"

    The improvement between A and B is the measurable, quantified contribution
    of the entire Stage 2 neural network architecture.

SECTION 6: WHY DID WE ACHIEVE THESE RESULTS?
================================================================================

6.1  Why RÂ² Jumped from 0.49 â†’ 0.61 (the +12.3% value-add)
--------------------------------------------------------------
    The Stage 1 baseline (RÂ²=0.49) can only explain city-level average
    pollution. It has no awareness of WHEN within a day pollution spikes.

    The Stage 2 jump to RÂ²=0.61 comes entirely from the model learning
    hourly meteorological triggers. Specifically:

    a) inv_PBLH is the single most powerful predictor of the timing and
       magnitude of pollution spikes. The BiLSTM learned that when inv_PBLH
       rises steeply in the morning (boundary layer collapse), the ratio
       output should spike dramatically. The ratio array above shows this
       perfectly: the model ramps from 0.63x at night to 6.72x by T+11,
       which is exactly when real boundary layers collapse in Indian winters.

    b) U_wind and V_wind allow the model to track whether pollution is being
       advected from industrial or residential zones. A wind direction shift
       toward an upwind industrial area correlates with a ratio > 1.0. This
       is directionally-aware pollution tracking that no standard scalar
       wind speed feature can provide.

    c) The ResidualAttention mechanism correctly identifies the high-variance
       timesteps (T+9 to T+13 in the extreme event) and assigns them higher
       weighting during sequence modelling, making the BiLSTM focus its
       representational capacity on the critical prediction hours.

6.2  Why the fMAE Loss Solved Peak-Smoothing
----------------------------------------------
    The core reason standard models miss extreme peaks: MSE creates a gradient
    landscape where the "safe" prediction (near-mean) has a lower expected loss
    than correctly predicting an extreme value. The model is mathematically
    incentivised to not bother with peaks.

    The Frequency-Weighted MAE inverts this. A PM2.5 > 400 event is so rare
    that its bin weight (via the effective-number formula) is orders of magnitude
    larger than a common 30-60 Âµg/mÂ³ event. The model receives massive gradient
    penalties for getting extreme peaks wrong, forcing it to learn those patterns.

    The added Variance Penalty (0.3 Ã— |std(pred) - std(true)|) specifically
    targets the "flat-line" failure mode: even if the weighted MAE is satisfied,
    the model cannot output a flat sequence because the variance term directly
    penalises a mismatch in signal amplitude. This is why the ratio array shows
    genuine diurnal structure (0.63x â†’ 6.72x â†’ 0.80x) rather than a flat 1.0x.

6.3  Why the Multiplicative Architecture is Correct
-----------------------------------------------------
    An additive approach (y_pred = baseline + neural_offset) would have the
    neural network working in physical space, fighting against the scale of
    the baseline. On a day when baseline is 300 Âµg/mÂ³ and actual is 900, the
    network must output an offset of 600 â€” a large raw number the loss function
    penalises heavily.

    The multiplicative approach (y_pred = ratio Ã— baseline) normalises the
    problem. The network always predicts a dimensionless scale factor. On a
    300 Âµg/mÂ³ baseline day, predicting a ratio of 3.0 is a small, bounded
    prediction. The network learns the SHAPE of the diurnal cycle (ratios
    oscillating around 1.0), not the absolute magnitude. This is physically
    natural: the satellite gives the magnitude, the network gives the shape.

6.4  Why RMSE Improved More than MAE (3.40 vs 0.83 Âµg/mÂ³ improvement)
------------------------------------------------------------------------
    RMSE is sensitive to large errors (because it squares them). The fact that
    RMSE improved 4Ã— more than MAE tells us specifically that Stage 2 is
    reducing the large catastrophic misses that Stage 1 makes at peak hours.
    The overall average (MAE) improves modestly, but the worst-case errors
    (what RMSE captures) improve dramatically. This is exactly the behaviour
    we engineered for with the fMAE loss and is directly relevant to the
    public health use case â€” we most care about not missing the dangerous events.

SECTION 7: LIMITATIONS & HONEST DISCLOSURE
================================================================================

7.1  Meteorologically Driven Peaks Only
-----------------------------------------
    The model predicts peaks caused by physical atmospheric mechanisms
    (PBLH collapse, wind direction, temperature inversion). It CANNOT predict:
        - Sudden industrial accidents or equipment failures
        - Diwali firecrackers or other one-off festival events
        - Previously unseen dust storm patterns from new source regions
    This limitation must be stated clearly in any publication.

7.2  Forecast vs. Observed Weather Input
------------------------------------------
    The model was trained on OBSERVED historical weather (ground truth).
    In real-world deployment, the input would be a 24-hour WEATHER FORECAST
    (e.g., from Open-Meteo's free forecast API). Weather forecast errors
    (Â±1-2Â°C temperature, Â±10-15% wind speed) will propagate into the
    PM2.5 forecast. The model's accuracy in production will therefore be
    slightly lower than the evaluation results shown here.

7.3  Zero-Shot RÂ² of 0.61 vs 0.91 In-Domain
----------------------------------------------
    The 30-point RÂ² gap between in-domain and zero-shot performance is
    expected and honest. The model has never seen Lucknow or Patna's
    specific emission source mix, local topography effects, or city-specific
    pollution patterns. The RÂ²=0.61 figure represents genuine generalization.

7.4  Station Count
--------------------
    The training set uses 8 station-chunks across 4 cities. A larger, more
    geographically diverse training corpus would improve zero-shot generalization.
    This is the primary direction for future work.
